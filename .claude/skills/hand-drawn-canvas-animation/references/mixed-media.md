# Continuity across materials

Use this guide when the brief asks for several styles or animation techniques
in one coherent work. A style describes marks and surfaces; a technique
describes how the image changes. Ink and pencil are looks. Whole drawings,
sand displacement, photo masking and hinged paper are different processes.
Found-motion tracing is a source of movement, not another surface treatment.
Count only the techniques actually used when describing a finished film.

## Give the changes a cause

Write the character's goal and the consequence of each action before assigning
materials. Decide what crosses each boundary: a pose, a screen position, a
direction of travel, a mark or an object. Then choose how the next material
advances that action. Do not require every film to use every available engine.

Useful connections from [Becoming](../examples/becoming-phoenix/phoenix.html):

- Wind breaks an ink world into grains, and a hand rebuilds the bird in sand.
- A drawn bird uses a real photographed lantern as shelter and a perch.
- Each jump advances to another ledge and leaves ink impressions. The camera
  follows the progression instead of resetting the bird for each material.
- The final printed frame is reused as the texture of the next book spread.
  Pulling the camera back reveals that it was a page; the page then turns.
- A folded figure rises from the gutter, opens its wings and fans its tail.
  Its grown silhouette carries into the next treatment.
- The original nest returns in the ending. Different feather materials belong
  to one bird within one landscape.

These are examples of continuity, not a mandatory sequence or transition menu.
Hard cuts are valid when the action and composition make the relationship clear.
A decorative wipe alone cannot explain why a character changed place or size.

## Make the looks visibly different

Design representation, line weight, fill density, negative space and surface
behaviour for each medium. Pencil can remain open and light while ink uses
larger black masses and white gaps. Separate the colour plates for riso and
use opaque shapes for screen print. In photo work, arrange the action around
the object's real edges and occlusion.
Changing colour or laying dots over the same finished drawing is often too weak.

A small side-by-side board checks whether the material differences read.
It does not check story continuity. Review the sequence with sound off and
watch whether the viewer can follow the same action across those differences.
Materials may also coexist within a shot, as long as the staging stays clear.

## Stage transitions on one timebase

Keep shot start times, action beats and audio cues on one timeline. If an
outgoing and incoming image overlap, render them into separate cleared layers;
preserve each scene's local time and clamp it at its authored boundaries.
Maintain world-space texture identities through the transition.

Inspect the last readable outgoing pose, the middle of the transition and the
first readable incoming pose. Check direction, scale, gaze, contacts and camera
motion. Use a designed replacement drawing or an occluding material change
when silhouettes no longer correspond; an arbitrary morph is not an inbetween.

For a print-to-page transition, use the actual completed image on the sheet.
For an unfolding object, follow [paper3d.md](paper3d.md). For storm, water or
wind-driven action, follow [motion.md](motion.md#environmental-force).

## Review the finished edit

Check several consecutive frames across the fastest action and each difficult
material change, then the encoded scene at normal speed. Watch for a character
resetting its progress, scenery that ignores the action, a finished prop
appearing at full size, and colour patches merging into an unintended silhouette.
Sound can reinforce a visible action but should not be needed to understand it.

Distinguish full rendering and automated playback/decode from visual and audio
review. Record which one was actually performed. A successful technical check
does not establish artistic quality.
